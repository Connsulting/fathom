# Backdoor Roth contributions and conversions

Tax year: 2026

Reviewed: 2026-11-05

## Rule

A backdoor Roth is a planning label for a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution followed by a Roth conversion. The contribution still requires eligible compensation and shares the regular IRA contribution limit. A conversion is a separate reportable event; direct Roth contribution income limits do not by themselves prohibit a conversion. A conversion does not create a second annual contribution allowance. [IRS Publication 590-A, IRA contributions and conversions](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590a) [IRS Form 8606 instructions, IRA basis and conversions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606)

## Review

For 2026, the regular IRA limit is $7,500 plus a $1,100 age-50 catch-up. Confirm how much has already been contributed across regular IRAs and whether any deduction was claimed. Preserve nondeductible basis on Form 8606 and report the conversion in its actual tax year. A contribution designated for an earlier year does not move a later conversion into that earlier year. [IRS Notice 2025-67, 2026 retirement limits](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-25-67.pdf) [IRS Publication 590-A, IRA contributions and conversions](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590a) [IRS Form 8606 instructions, IRA basis and conversions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606)

## Trap and interaction

A separate IRA used for the transaction does not isolate its basis. Traditional IRA, traditional SEP IRA and traditional SIMPLE IRA balances can make part of the conversion taxable under the pro-rata rules. Earnings can also be taxable. Review the Form 8606 pro-rata entry before assuming any conversion is tax free, including when a self-employed business funds a SEP. [IRS Form 8606 instructions, IRA basis and conversions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606)
